Abstract

Permutations are everywhere, it seems, in mathematics and computer science, and even in the social sciences. In many cases, for instance, when you are interested in sorting, it is natural to think of the set of permutations on a set of n elements as a probability space. Suppose you have information about some job candidates that suggests some are better than others, but nothing directly comparing x and y. What is the probability that x is better than y? Does it necessarily go up if you get a new letter saying x is better than z?

Suppose you wish to sort a set of data, but comparisons are expensive. Which pair of elements, if compared, would give you the most information?

This course will take you to the world of partial orders, linear extensions, permutahedra, permutation patterns, sorting algorithms, exact and approximate counting problems, random walk, and limit structures. You will need to have taken at least a basic course in probability (such as MATH 20) and seen enough mathematics or theoretical computer science to be comfortable with abstract concepts and logical reasoning.

There will be a homework assignment due at the beginning of each class, usually just one problem. You may consult with your colleagues or AI on homework provided they are suitably credited, but neither will be available during exams. You will not be required to write computer programs, but writing and running a program will sometimes provide an alternative way to do an assignment.

Here is a rough weekly syllabus:

1. The probability space of permutations: fixed points, cycles

2. Partial orders and linear extensions: permutahedron, convex subsets

3. Probability and sorting: correlation, XYZ inequality

4. The 1/3-2/3 conjecture and its successors

5. MCMC and approximate counting of linear extensions

6. #P hardness and exact counting of linear extensions

7. Sorting algoithms: quicksort, merging, filing

8. Stack-sorting, permutation patterns, Marcus-Tardos Theorem

9. Permutations generated by random walk

10. Limit structures, permutons

Assignments

Due Wednesday April 1: Let a permutation of {1,2,...,n} be chosen uniformly at random. For n large, what is the probability that it contains a cycle of length greater than n/2?

Due Friday April 3: Write up a little theorem and proof to justify the claim that, in the 100 prisoners problem, no matter how the warden chooses to distribute names in boxes, if the prisoners assign ownership of boxes uniformly at random, then the resulting permutation (mapping each prisoner to the name found inside the box he owns) is uniformly random.

Due Monday April 6: See if you can fix the insertion algorithm---keeping the spirit of the name---so that its worst-case running time is better than Θ(n2).

Due Wednesday April 8: A classic puzzle presents you with 12 coins, one of which is counterfeit and is either lighter or heaver than the others. The task is to determine which coin is the counterfeit and whether it is lighter or heavier, using just three weighings with a balance scale. Your mission, however, is to prove that it can't be done with 14 coins or (for your second point) even 13.

Due Friday April 10: Let x0, x1, x2, x3, x4 be a sequence of integers with positive sum, with indices interpreted modulo 5. An "operation" consists of selecting a negative term, say xk, replacing xk by -xk (a positive number), replacing xk-1 by xk-1 + xk, and replacing xk+1 by xk+1 + xk. Now, given any sequence x0, x1, x2, x3, x4, you can create a doubly-infinite sequence ... s-1 s0 s1 with the property that sn+1 = sn + xn mod 5. The question is: what does an "operation" on x0, x1, x2, x3, x4 do to its corresponding infinite sequence?

Due Wednesday April 15: Suppose you start with n items in a row. You choose one of the items uniformly at random and remove that item along with all the items to its left or all to its right, whichever is the smaller set. For example, if n = 10 and you choose item 8, items 8 through 10 are removed; but if you choose item 4, items 1 through 4 are removed. You now repeat this procedure on the remaining items, and repeat again, etc., until all the items are gone. How many "procedures" does this require, on average?

Due Friday April 17: Consider the following algorithm, which sorts "in place" but not by doing comparisons; instead, the items are numbered 1 through n and the spaces likewise. The algorithm finds (nondeterministically) an item which is not where it belongs, and shoves it in where it belongs---shifting the items in between up or down by one. For example, suppose item number 8 is found in space number 4. That item is moved into the 8th position, and the items previously in positions 5,6,7, and 8 are shifted down to positions 4,5,6, and 7 respectively. Note that this could dislodge some items that were previously in the right spot. Your question is: Does this algorithm even work? Clearly, if and when the algorithm terminates, the items will be correctly sorted. But maybe, if the algorithm makes sufficiently poor choices, it can run forever. Experiment with the algorithm and try to justify your answer.

Due Monday April 20: Draw the Hasse diagram of the state poset for the above algorithm of the last homework, in the case of 4 items. The states for this algorithm are the 4! permutations of {1,2,3,4}, with 1234 as the target and therefore the unique minimal element.

Due Wednesday April 22: Let P be a finite poset of height h(P) (= size of longest chain) and width w(P) (= size of biggest antichain). Show that |P| (the number of elements of P) cannot exceed the product h(P)w(P).

Due Thursday April 23: You need a gallon of paint from the automatic paint dispenser at your local hardward store, which you are supposed to get by selecting a color, putting an empty gallon can under the spigot, and pushing a button. Today, however, instead of dispensing a gallon of paint, the machine dispenses a uniformly random amount of paint between 0 and 1 gallon. (Luckily, an electric eye shuts off the flow instantly when the can is full.) Guess (and bring in your guess, in your head) the average number of times you will need to push that button to get your gallon can filled.

Due Monday April 27: If you are a CS major or CS grad student, simulate Thursday's problem and report (on paper) your estimate of the expected number of times the paint machine button needs to be pushed. If you are a math major or math grad student, try to derive the exact answer (a hint will be provided at Thursday's class). If you belong to zero or two of the above categories, it's your choice.

Due Wednesday April 29: Draw the Hasse diagram for each of the 16 isomorphism classes of 4-element posets, and for each indicate the number of different labellings, i.e., the number of labelled posets with that diagram. (You get this by dividing 4! by the number of symmetries of the diagram.) The sum of these 16 numbers should be 219.

Due Friday May 1: Recall that a poset is graded if all of its maximal chains are the same length. But our intuition is that "graded" means it has levels. Show that P is graded, with height h, iff it can be partitioned into levels L1 through Lh so that the minimal elements are in L1, maximal in Lh, and all coverings (that is, all edges of the Hasse diagram) are between consecutive levels.

Due Wednesday May 6: Recall that the comparability graph CG(P) of a poset P puts x~y (that is, x is adjacent to y) iff x < y or y < x in P. The complement IG(P) of CG(P) has edges wherever CG(P) doesn't, so in IG(P), x~y iff x||y in P, i.e., iff x and y are incomparable. Recall also that the intersection of two linear orderings of the same set of elements is the poset (again on the same set of elements) in which x < y iff x < y in both orderings. If the orderings are written as permutations using one-line notation, that means x appears to the left of y in both permutations.

Prove "Allison's Theorem," to wit: IG(P) is itself the comparability graph of some poset iff P has dimension 2 or less, i.e., if there exist two (possibly equal) linear orderings (permutations) of the elements of P whose intersection is P.

(Scoring: 1 point for each direction (=> or <=) of your proof.)

Due Monday May 11: Let P be the height-2 poset consisting of minimal elements a1, . . . , an and maximal elements b1, . . . , bn with the relations ai < bj iff i is not equal to j. Show that the dimension of P is exactly n.

Due Wednesday May 13: Describe the permutations that avoid the pattern 321.

Due Friday May 15: Devise a sorting machine that works iff the permutation to be sorted is 321-avoiding. Use your machine to show that the number of 321-avoiding permutations of order n is the nth Catalan number (i.e., the number of Dyck paths of length 2n).

Due Monday May 18: Compute the pattern densities for all 6 patterns of length 3 for the permutation 1324, and for a permutation σ of your choice in S5 that you think might be close to 1324. Then compute how close they are with respect to patterns of length 3, by computing the total variation between the two probability distributions on S3 that the pattern densities give you for 1324 and σ. (Recall that the total variation distance dTV between two discrete probability distributions is half the sum of the absolute difference between their values on individual outcomes, and is thus some number between 0 and 1.)

Due Wednesday May 20: Prove that for any class C of permutations there's a set A of permutations (patterns) such that C is the set of permutations that avoid every pattern in A, and moreover, A is an antichain (no pattern in A is found inside any other pattern in A). Is A unique to C? Why or why not?

Due Friday May 22: Describe a random sequence of points on [0,1] with each of the following properties: (1) It converges to a certain point with probability 1; (2) It converges with probability 1, but not always to the same point; (3) With probability 1 it fails to converge, but the distributions do converge; (4) Neither the points nor their distributions converge.

Due Wednesday May 27: Compute pattern densities of length 3 for the permuton which is uniform on each of the two squares [0,c] x [0,c] and {c,1] x [c,1]. (The six probabilities you get for this will, presumably, each depend on c.) HINT: Condition on the number of samples in each square. BUT: Instead of computing them you are welcome to estimate them by simulation, for various values of c.

Due Friday May 29: Compute the expected number of comparisons needed to do binary search on a list of 5 items, (a) starting with item 2 or item 4, and (b) starting with item 3. It is assumed that the desired item is present, and initially equally likely to be in any position.

Due Monday June 1: If σ is a permutation of order m and τ is a permutation of order n, define σ ⊕ τ to be the permutation of order m+n whose 1-line representation is σ1 σ2 . . . σm m+τ1 . . . m+τn. Define σ ⊖ τ to be the permutation of order n+m whose 1-line representation is n+σ1 . . . n+σm; τ1 . . . τn. Let C be the set of permutations defined in the following recursive manner: The permutation 1 is in C, and if σ and τ are in C, so are σ ⊕ τ and σ ⊖ τ. Show that C is a "permutation class" by exhibiting, with proof, an antichain of patterns whose avoidance defines C.

Due Wednesday June 3: Describe and draw the permutons that correspond to the large permutations whose comparability graphs are (a) the complete balanced bipartite graph, and (b) the complete balanced tripartite graph. (These permutons would represent the two cusps at the low end of the right-hand border of the scalloped triangle.)

Honor Code

Students are permitted to work together to do homework problems. What is important is a student's eventual understanding of homework problems, and not how that is achieved. The honor principle applies to homework in the following way. What a student turns in as a written homework solution is to be his or her own understanding of how to do the problem. Students must state what sources they have consulted, with whom they have collaborated, and from whom they have received help. Students are discouraged from using solutions to problems that may be posted on the web, and as just stated, must reference them if they use them. The solutions you submit must be written by you alone. Any copying (electronic or otherwise) of another person's solutions, in whole or in part, is a violation of the Honor Code.

If you employ generative AI, e.g. ChatGPT, say so, and say how you verified that what it told you is correct.

If you have any questions as to whether some action would be acceptable under the Academic Honor Code, please speak to me, and I will be glad to help clarify things. It is always easier to ask beforehand than to have trouble later!
